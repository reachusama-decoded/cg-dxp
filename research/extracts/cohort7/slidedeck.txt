D1.3 Three Views of a Data Product

1. Alignment: aligned to a purpose or goal; phrased and structured for it
2. Asset: data product = code + infra + data
3. Promises: data product = data set + promises
data set + (SLAs + ownership + governance + quality + …)

One thing, three questions: aligned to whom, made of what, promising what

The views describe one thing from different angles, and they reconcile: most promises are automated by code
(promises meet asset), and alignment is a promise that the product will meet your needs (alignment meets promises).
The promises view does the most work this week.

D1.4 Source-Aligned → Consumer-Aligned

Each step reshapes the data away from the source’s format and towards the consumer’s question

• source = THEM: structured for the purposes of whoever produced it
• consumer = US: structured for the question our consumer needs answered
• the data creator sits in the middle and does the realignment

Morningstar’s business is book-of-record data for stock-market events; our customers need it grouped by funds and
portfolio managers. Morningstar doesn’t have our customers, so its data isn’t shaped for them. Realigning it is the
data product. The terms are relative: source-aligned to them, consumer-aligned to us.

D1.5 What Do Data Products Promise?

A promise is an obligation between two parties.

Each promise becomes machinery you will build or configure this week

A data product is not a data set, and not a property of a data set: it consists principally in the promises. When we ship
one, we release something that will have a certain quality, arrive at a certain time, and have an owner who responds
to emails. Otherwise it’s a dump of data, and no one cares about that.

D1.15 What is AI?

Artificial Intelligence: an attempt to solve problems with machines that presently require
human intelligence.
Two kinds matter today:

• discriminative = labelling existing content
• generative = generating new content

LLM, Large Language Model: an LLM is a model of language.

Spam filters and fraud flags are discriminative; Catalyst is generative. And language itself is a very low-information
system (the symbols T-R-E-E have nothing to do with a tree, no bark, no green), so it is remarkable that analysing
text alone learns anything at all.
DECODED
17

D1.16 How Does an LLM Model Language?

The compression is the model: predicting your next word is decompressing the corpus

• trained on as close to every sentence ever written as exists: the internet
• the training compresses that text; the compression is the probability model

Ideally the vendors would train on every sentence ever written: predicting your next word would then just be looking
up this conversation in the book of everything ever said. The internet is the substitute: a bad choice, but the biggest
available.
DECODED
18

D1.17 How Does a Modern LLM Work?

Compression gives the model knowledge; refinement makes it follow instructions and preferences

1. Pre-training: compress the corpus (c. 2017–2020, “training”)
2. Instruction fine-tuning: thousands of annotators write instruction/response pairs
3. RLHF: experts rank pairs of responses; the model learns the preferences

Why stage 3? There are near-infinite ways to follow an instruction correctly but badly: if told to bake a cake, burning
the cake is still baking a cake. Stages 2 and 3 are now called post-training, and stage 1 pre-training, which tells you
where the focus has moved.
DECODED

D1.18 Talking to an LLM

response = llm(conversation)

• non-deterministic: the same prompt can give different completions
• known failure modes: hallucination, inconsistency

The prompt is the entire conversation so far: nothing else is remembered

As a conversation grows, the prompt becomes the entire conversation: the input is always everything so far. You can’t
solve non-determinism, but you can tame it with reference prompts, crafted and tested until the output is reliably
roughly right. A Catalyst skill is exactly that, curated.
DECODED

D1.19 Why AI Is Unusually Good at Code

Code is the one domain where preferences can be automated: the computer itself judges
whether the programme runs and the output is right.

The gap between where LLMs work well and where real products live is two orders of magnitude
Maths, recipes and poetry need a human to say “this one’s better”; code can be reinforced over billions of examples.
That’s why Catalyst is reliable for data-product code. The line counts are the caveat: engineers use LLMs to generate
parts of systems and stay involved in producing the whole.

D1.21 How the Tooling Works: the AI Harness, Models, Effort

An LLM only generates text. The harness (Copilot) wraps it: observes the response,
interprets tool calls, runs them, feeds results back.

• Agent vs Plan mode: generate-and-run vs say-what-you-would-do
• Approvals: default · autopilot · bypass (dangerous)

D2.3 Effective AI: What Hallucination Really Is

In an LLM there is no second mechanism: the model is always doing the same thing,
computing a completion from internet data, instruction data, and preference data.

• no mode switch between “telling the truth” and “making things up”
• the mechanism that generates correct tokens is the one that generates incorrect ones
• so nothing inside an LLM can make it “not hallucinate”

In that sense LLMs are always hallucinating; sometimes you like the output and sometimes you don’t. Whenever we
work with these systems we should simply expect this behaviour, which is why review is a design requirement rather
than a nicety.
DECODED
28

D2.4 Context-Window Bias

The model places a learned importance distribution over the prompt, and it over-weights
the start and the end; attention to the middle fades.

• as the prompt grows, your important information ends up in the middle
• instruction words (“write”, “draw”) get enormous early weight
• the entire conversation is always the input; a new conversation resets it

Like handing someone a book and asking what it’s about: they read the first page and the last page and skim the
middle. If a chatbot seems to remember across threads, that’s the harness using hidden prompts, memories, or
retrieval, not the model.
DECODED
29

D2.5 When to Be Skeptical

1. Long conversations: expect contradictions with earlier content
2. Novelty: your paved path applied to your Morningstar dataset is unlikely to be
documented anywhere the model has seen

And a caveat with teeth: AI models can report work inaccurately. A model may claim
everything works while every function returns its arguments.

A productive workflow: let a long generation complete, then treat the result as a prototype to test for errors before
restarting. Avoid the casino mindset of repeating the same generation hoping the next result improves by chance.
And never assess what a model did from the conversation alone: inspect the files and run the tests.
DECODED
30

D2.6 Tips for Using AI Models Well

1. Use the model to critique itself (or cross models): drafting and critiquing activate
different weights
2. Plan, and plan to plan: never “go and do X”; ask for the plan first
3. Understand steering: an LLM cannot ignore your words; add dangerous specifics late
4. State the future use of an output (“later I’ll use this for Y; don’t do Y now”)
5. Borrow the model’s metacognition: “what might I not know here?”
6. Know when to start fresh: a new conversation that subtracts

Steering is why skills work: a skill is a handcrafted steering system, key words placed to pull the model toward
the right completion. A human can challenge or ignore a misplaced detail; the model averages it in, so sometimes
removing words helps as much as adding them.

D2.9 Prioritising Promises: RAID

Risks · Assumptions · Issues · Dependencies: assessing the degree to which you can keep
your promises.

Assumptions is the quadrant that matters most: it holds whatever your SLAs silently depend on

We only promise to meet our SLAs if upstream providers meet theirs, which is why the assumptions row matters

D2.10 Prioritising Promises: MoSCoW and RICE

MoSCoW ranks categories and stalls at should; RICE ranks the individual trade-off when one promise has to give

The formulas are comparative: all that matters is that they rank the promises correctly, and
that you keep the notes.

The value is accountability and transparency rather than better cognition: in a large financial organisation you must
account for decisions through an audit trail, and “why did you decide that?” is harder to answer well on the spot
than the decision was to make. Worked case: being ten minutes late on the timing SLA to deliver the explanation,
defensibly.

D2.11 Adoption is a Leadership Problem

• Management: given a policy, organise the business to execute it
• Leadership: set the mission, stay close to where people are, bridge the gap
• Innovation: out ahead of everyone, principled, stubborn, and frightening

You lead change you cannot mandate: influence goes up your chain, across, and down theirs

Innovation and management don’t solve adoption problems: innovators demonstrate practices miles from where
people are, and management just tells people what to do. Often the leader’s real job is to pick an innovator they trust
and bring people along that path. These five days are themselves an adoption exercise for the paved path.

The standard Capital tooling for data products:
Tool                                   Plain-English job

D2.16 Git and GitHub

D2.17 dbt in One Slide

dbt (data build tool): data transforms as SQL + templates + tests.
This is the tool that performs the source-to-consumer alignment.
Q. How do you test SQL?

A test is a query for rows that must never exist: zero rows is a pass

A dbt test is just another query, written so that it returns rows only when something is wrong. Zero rows means the
test passes. A core part of dbt testing is writing queries that should never produce data.

D2.18 Airflow and Databricks in One Slide

Airflow: an orchestrator. Think of a conductor: while the orchestra plays, the conductor is
timing, cueing, and coordinating. Airflow times, cues, and coordinates tasks.
Databricks runs the rules:

• dbt = rules to transform data
• Databricks = runs those rules on data inside files & folders (AWS S3)

For Linux people, Airflow is cron; for everyone else, it’s Windows Task Scheduler. And the strong argument for
Databricks today is vendor integration: Morningstar and Bloomberg will simply drop files into the folders. With a
traditional database, vendors would need credentials and insert access to our systems.

D2.21 Docker in Depth: Two Roles, Four Layers

Docker plays two roles on the paved path: development (bundle dependencies so code
runs anywhere) and release (to make software live is to deploy the container).

Each Dockerfile line adds a layer: the image is a recipe, executed bottom to top

absurdly expensive way to get 5, so the commands are worth the overhead: a dbt run, an Airflow server.

D3.2 The Line: Development vs Live

Above the line, the product is essentially done: dbt, Docker, Git, GitHub (the code, the
container, the record). Below the line it runs:

• Databricks: the release target; where products are discovered and read
• Airflow: triggers the product’s code to run
• Harness: the wrapper around all of it; a data creator’s detail can stop here

What you will mostly be doing is writing dbt code.

The working loop is short: you receive a container, a dbt project and sometimes Airflow configuration; you modify the
dbt code, create the STTM that records the rules, and push the change. Python’s absence from the list is deliberate:
a data creator can often avoid writing Python altogether.

D3.4 Demo: Anatomy of a Dockerfile

Four instructions are a complete container: FROM chooses the base image, WORKDIR and COPY bring the code in,
CMD names the one command the container exists to run

Each line produces a layer, and layers are cached: change your code and only COPY onwards rebuilds, which is why
builds after the first take seconds. The production Astro Dockerfile is the same idea with CG certificates and registries
baked in.

D3.8 Data Systems: Database, Warehouse, Lake, Lakehouse

The loop: files, then databases for the guarantees, then files again for the vendors

• database: structured (= tabular) storage; a relation is a table
• warehouse: an analytical (OLAP) database that also tracks history
• data store / lake: files & folders; flexible formats (Parquet), low cost
• one trend across all of it: increasing flexibility of storage

“Structured always means tabular” in this discipline, confusingly, since an image is obviously structured too; that’s
the history of the field. When you hear “data store”, you can almost always just think files and folders.

D3.9 OLTP vs OLAP: Rows vs Columns

The layout follows the workload: append rows to write fast, group columns to read fast

The original OLTP databases were retail databases, and the transactions were literal financial transactions; today the
word means any write that must succeed or fail as a whole. Rule of thumb: write-heavy databases have few columns
and many rows; analytical databases have many columns, parallelise well, and you very rarely see a GROUP BY in a
transactional database.
DECODED

D3.10 The Lakehouse, and ACID

A lakehouse reimplements relational-database capabilities on top of a data lake: the
storage stays files (Parquet); the querying layer adds SQL and ACID.
ACID together = linearization under failure: write 1, 2, 3 and, whatever fails in between, you
read back 1, 2, 3 or you’re told it failed.

Almost all databases had ACID until roughly 2005; the distributed decade relaxed the guarantees for speed, everyone
rediscovered why they mattered, and lakehouses added them back. Try it yourself: DESCRIBE HISTORY on any Delta
table shows every write as a version, and VERSION AS OF reads an earlier state; that versioning is how ACID lives on
plain files.

Every group-by moves you left: maximum granularity is generally at the source

D3.13 What is an STTM?

The data lineage from source to target: the business rules and transformational logic that
take you from one to the other.
e.g. individual accounts: two to four sources JOIN → dedup → assign a unique internal
ID

How do you specify one? In English: source attributes and definitions (a “glossary”), target
attributes and types, and the rules between them.

That unique-ID step is one of the genuinely hard areas. Think of police databases: names spelled differently across
crime-scene reports, and no easy way to know whether two reports describe the same person (the “golden nominal”
problem). Morningstar, Bloomberg and the rest each use their own IDs, and deciding whether they mean the same
asset is the same problem.

D3.15 The Gap Between English and Code

Where does an English STTM go wrong?

• “pick a unique value”: which policy? whose?
• vague requirements: “firm name”, legal or parent?
• null handling: what does null mean here? emptiness? zero-ness? defaults?
• complex conditions: does or mean either, or possibly both?
• scope of terms: “phone number”, US only or worldwide?

Every bullet is a real bug class, and the fix is a question back to the requirement’s owner, asked before building. On
nulls: for every kind of data there is a different meaning of empty. English is ambiguous, so add words rather than
relying on “if” and “or”.

D3.17 SLAs, SLIs, SLOs, and RAID

• SLA, service level agreement: a promise between two parties
• SLI, service level indicator: the quantitative measure tracked
• SLO, service level objective: if the indicator were perfect, its value

Measure it (SLI), target it (SLO), promise it with a tolerance (SLA)

Other examples: 7pm delivery or a P1 call-out · recovery time · a “duty to explain”.

The “duty to explain”: during the pandemic, oil futures went negative, which had never happened in recorded history.
A trader seeing that number needs someone on the hook to say whether it’s real or a data-quality issue. For scored
risks, RAID multiplies probability by impact (1 to 5 each); 16 or above needs immediate mitigation.

D3.18 What Does 99.9% Actually Mean?

Each nine buys a factor of ten: 99.9% still allows 8.76 hours down per year, about a working day

Saying “three nines” is saying “we can be down on the order of a day a year”. For calibration: Claude’s availability is
about one nine, optimistically, and GitHub’s has fallen to roughly one nine too. Industry standard for serious systems
is three to five.

D3.21 How Does DBT Work?

dbt defines data transformations in mostly-SQL syntax, with extensions, and provides a
testing approach.

• simplifies SQL down to just SELECT: each file’s query defines a table named after the file
• the {{ … }} braces are the extensions, and they are always replaced by SQL

Two ways of connecting models: ref (“this depends on that model”; dbt builds the dependency tree and runs every-
thing upstream) and source (“this depends on data dbt does not build”). Refs are what dbt chains together; sources
are what must already exist.

D4.7 How Does Airflow Work?

What problem does it solve? Orchestration: starting, stopping, timing tasks.

• DAG: the structured group of tasks, plus its scheduling
• Task: a programme; a group of operators
• Operator: the adaptor to another system (dbt, SQL, even Google Docs)
• Scheduler: runs the DAGs

The whiteboard DAG and the Python are the same drawing: a sequence on success, a fan-out on failure

Airflow stole the term DAG: everything is a directed acyclic graph, dbt’s model tree included, but in data engineering
“DAG” now just means an Airflow task sequence. In practice, using Airflow comes down to understanding your
operators; the rest is rote configuration you look up.

D5.3 How Does a Data Product Get Released?

Continuous Integration (“merging”) · Continuous Deployment

• continuous = as-and-when the quality checks pass, usually daily or weekly

The cadence is the feedback loop: months of drift, or days

The three-month cycle genuinely works for one to three developers. In larger teams, by the time you release, other
teams and their understanding have moved, and some of what you built is no longer valid. Releasing often keeps the
shared understanding current, so mistakes surface fast.

D5.8 Challenge 5.2: The 3 a.m. Failure Drill

The nightly DAG failed a dependency check; the contract promises data by 7:30. Trace the
failure backwards through the stack (20 to 30 minutes, groups, on paper):

• Airflow task logs: did it run, and which task failed?
• Databricks job/query history: did the SQL fail, on which query?
• Harness execution history: did someone deploy last night?
• Datadog: which monitor shows the breach, and who was alerted?

Then draft the duty-to-notify message.

A failure the morning after a release points first at the release. The message states who is told, by when, what is
known, what remains unknown, and when the next update comes. Data creators often read these systems rather
than operate them; the drill connects an SLA promise to the evidence.

D5.14 Recording Decisions: the RFD (Challenge 5.4)

An RFD needs three sections: Context (constraints and a real alternative), Decision,
Consequences (including at least one negative, with its mitigation).
Challenge 5.4: write one from your build, until a partner could implement the decision
without asking what you meant.

When reviewing an RFD, a Consequences section containing only benefits is grounds for requesting changes: the
author must name what the team gives up and which mitigation makes that cost acceptable. Otherwise a later
reader cannot distinguish an evaluated decision from a preferred option written after the fact.

Daily learning objectives and teaching summaries

Day 1 Product discovery and effective AI use
Learning objectives
By the end of the day, learners should be able to:

• Explain what makes a dataset a trusted, reusable product for a particular consumer.
• Justify a product through its purpose, value, feasibility, cost, and adoption needs.
• Interview producers and consumers, then generate and check a PRD and data contract
using Catalyst.
• Use AI settings and context deliberately, evaluate outputs, and identify lifecycle phases
and build prerequisites.

Summary
Start with learners’ existing products and pain points, then examine business justification,
lifetime value, and adoption. Introduce Catalyst and effective AI practice before the
interview challenge, where learners turn a conversation into a checked PRD and data
contract. Close with the lifecycle and build prerequisites.

Product discovery and justification
Use the opening examples to establish that a product must serve a particular consumer
and purpose. Ask what promises make the data usable, who owns those promises, and
what enforces them.
For Challenge 2, ask each group to choose an opening example or propose a new CG
product. Give them these questions:

• What business problem or decision does it support, and who needs it?
• Does a suitable product already exist? What do people do today?
• Can we build it, and what would make the work difficult?
• Is the benefit worth the development and ongoing cost?
• Why would consumers adopt it? Why should it be prioritised now?

Have groups note their most difficult or surprising question. Early finishers can examine a
second product.
In the debrief, follow the product beyond an API or intermediate system to the person
making a decision. Use examples such as advisor/client master data, global differences in
definitions, and fragmented engagement data. Press for a clear consumer and business
owner.
Ask: “What changes for the person using this?” A platform name or API describes part of
the implementation; ask for the decision it supports. Each group should leave Challenge 2
with a named consumer, purpose and reason to proceed, plus one unresolved question.
When discussing lifetime value, include development, licences, data acquisition,
maintenance, SME time, coordination, training, and opportunity cost. Elicit benefits such as

Connect adoption barriers to practical responses: demonstrations, documentation, training,

Catalyst and independent prompting
Show how Catalyst combines reusable prompts and templates with scripts and lifecycle
workflows. Inspect a contract template, including identity, schema, service commitments,
and quality. Demonstrate a specific skill before introducing broader orchestration.
For Challenge 3, partners must use the same product but keep their descriptions private
until both PRDs are generated. Ask them to compare scope, consumers, requirements, and
assumptions. Draw out how input detail changes the result. Have each pair identify one
correction or additional piece of context they would provide. Pairs who finish first should
exchange one requirement with another pair and ask whether it is precise enough to check.
Bring their disagreement to the debrief.
Check: a tidy document can still contain invented fields, owners or approvals. Use the
planted contract errors if the live outputs do not give you a clear example. Keep
comparison to one or two differences so the activity finishes before lunch.

Effective AI practice
Teach the afternoon concepts in this order:

1. Intelligence, automation, and the distinction between discriminative and generative
systems.
2. Training data, prediction, instruction tuning, preference feedback, and checking
generated code.
3. Inference and intermediate reasoning.
4. Tool calls, the harness that executes them, agents, and Catalyst skills.
5. Model choice and the trade-offs between quality, speed, and cost.
6. Reasoning settings, context limits, compaction, and deliberate summaries before
starting a fresh conversation.
7. Evaluating performance on the learner’s actual task, particularly where internal
knowledge or novel requirements matter.

Keep the conceptual introduction brief enough to leave time for the interface
demonstration. Use a concrete product task to show how context, settings, and iteration
affect the output.

Interview challenge
For Challenge 4, form new groups of three:

• The producer knows the upstream source and its constraints.
• The consumer knows the decision, desired result, and working practices.
• The creator interviews both and reconciles their requirements.

Choose a familiar product. Give the creator these starting questions:

1. What decision do you make with the data?

preferences and actual practice.

criteria, then the contract’s schema, rules, quality, dependencies, glossary, and change
management. Include invented revision history among the errors learners should check.

prerequisites, and the skills supporting those prerequisites. Use 14:55–15:00 for each group
to share one correction and the evidence behind it. Check the answers against the current
organisational guidance. Keep the corrected PRD and contract for Day 2.
Before learners leave: save the product notes, interview record, reviewed PRD, contract and
open questions together. The team worksheet provides a place to record evidence,
assumptions, mappings and owners. Learners should be able to explain one correction they
made and why.

Day 2 Mappings service levels and implementation foundations
Learning objectives
By the end of the day, learners should be able to:

• Specify source-to-target transformations and measurable service commitments in a
data contract.
• Explain the roles of the tools and services in the paved path.
• Build and run a Docker image and work inside the development environment.
• Generate, explain, and investigate dbt and Airflow code from a contract.

Summary
Extend Day 1’s contract with mappings and service commitments, then improve its
presentation and resolve specification ambiguities. Explain the platform architecture,
practise Docker, and use the contract to generate and investigate dbt and Airflow
implementation.

Mappings and service commitments
Begin with source-to-target mapping (STTM): direct mapping, constants, lookups,
aggregation, and derived transformations. Use a small source-and-target example to show
joins and column rules. Retrieve the organisation’s specific transformation categories
through Catalyst.
Distinguish a measurable service-level indicator (SLI), its target (SLO), and the agreement
governing service commitments (SLA). Elicit latency, throughput, recovery time, delivery
delay and time zone, completeness, accuracy, freshness, support turnaround, and
availability. Discuss replication, failover, load balancing, and disaster recovery where they
help explain a commitment’s cost.
For Challenge 6:

1. Ask learners to query Catalyst individually about transformation types and service
measures.
2. In pairs or small groups, select a contract from Day 1.
3. Specify source and target tables, transformation rules, lookups, and realistic service
commitments.
4. Ask Catalyst to extend the contract, then inspect and revise the result.
5. Share the revised contract for review.

Use a submitted contract to demonstrate Markdown preview and editing. Generate a
self-contained HTML document with an overview, contents, explanatory prose, diagrams,
and navigation. Check whether the presentation makes the specification easier to review.
Return to mapping detail before moving on: resolve identifiers, keys, edge cases, null
meanings, and zero/minimum/maximum values.
Ask: “Could someone else implement this rule without asking you what it means?” For a
lookup, check the join key and what happens with zero or several matches. For a promise
such as “available by 07:30”, ask for the time zone, eligible days, measurement point and

action on failure. Finish Challenge 6 with at least one unambiguous mapping and one

Platform architecture and Docker
Trace the roles of Git, Catalyst, Docker, dbt, Databricks, S3, Airflow/Astronomer, and Unity
Catalog. Show where source data, transformations, compute, scheduling, version control,
and deployment fit. Explain the standard path and how to handle a justified exception.
Cover relational structure, transactions and ACID guarantees, distributed storage,
Hadoop/MapReduce, Spark, and Delta metadata and logs. Use this progression to explain
the training environment.
Keep the distinction clear: Git stores versions of code; Docker provides the execution
environment; dbt defines transformations and tests; Databricks executes SQL; Airflow
coordinates work; Unity Catalog governs and describes registered data. Use the paved-path
diagram rather than drawing the whole stack from memory.
In the Docker demonstration, cover:

• Dependencies, reproducibility, isolation, and shared host resources.
• Dockerfile → image → running container.
• Docker Desktop, port mappings, and resource settings.
• Reopening the repository in a development container and recognising its terminal.
• How the environment supports writing dbt and Airflow files, executing work, and
committing code.

For Challenge 7, use a short classroom version of the Docker project: run a prepared image,
inspect a shell inside it, complete a small Dockerfile, build it and run the result. Pull the
base image and prepare the supplied app before the session. The full foundation phase
takes 60–90 minutes; leave its additional tasks for further practice.
Ask: “Why did the container stop, and what survives when you run a fresh one?” Check
that learners distinguish the image, the running process and its writable files. Keep a
working example ready for anyone blocked by engine access. Pair learners who need
support; offer extensions to experienced participants.

Generate and investigate implementation
For Challenge 8, have learners:

1. Give Catalyst their data contract and request a dbt project.
2. Request simulated data matching the contract if source data is unavailable.
3. Add explanatory comments and obtain instructions for running the code and finding
outputs.
4. Generate Airflow orchestration matching the contract’s cadence or trigger.
5. Request explanations of the Airflow code and how to execute it.
6. Run what they can, inspect results, and prepare one discovery and one correction for
the debrief.

During Challenge 8, pairs who finish the first implementation should choose one further
question: how a missing input is handled, whether a lookup can duplicate rows, or what

• Find one assumption about keys, nulls, dates, or scheduling.
• Suggest a concrete check and say what should happen if it fails.

problems in the generated code.

Day 3 dbt practical and consolidation
Learning objectives
By the end of the day, learners should be able to:

• Work through the dbt pipeline exercises using the training environment.
• Explain models, dependencies, tests, and the impact of source or schema changes.
• Configure materialisation and connect dbt execution to storage and catalogue
conventions.
• Review generated artefacts and identify questions to resolve before the capstone.

Summary
Centre the day on the self-paced dbt challenges, with a guest session before lunch and
continued practical work afterwards. Use the closing recap to consolidate models,
dependencies, tests, storage, catalogues, and materialisation, and prepare the next day’s
guest contributions.

Practical facilitation and recap
Use the dbt project for the task sequence and completion criteria. Select the foundation
tasks first; advanced work has its own starter and time budget. Ask learners to keep a list
of commands, configuration changes, questions, and discoveries. Support different working
speeds without requiring everyone to stop at the same exercise.
As you circulate, check three things:

• Sources and staging: can the learner state what one row means, identify its key and
explain the types and units?
• Joins and models: are the lookup keys unique, do dates belong in the join, and what
happens to unmatched rows?
• Tests and output: can the learner show a value checked independently of the model
that produced it?

Ask: “Could this join pass its tests and still double the reported value?” Check the row
grain, right-side uniqueness, unmatched rows and an independent total. A successful build
alone is insufficient.
For the supplied Portfolio Analytics case, keep the measure precise: holdings-only GBP
value divided by 100 million synthetic units, with cash excluded. It is not official fund NAV.
Use the project’s reference checks for the exact results. The eight foundation questions
provide short explanations when a learner is stuck.
Lead the recap in this order:

1. Models and conventions: staging and integration models, CG naming conventions, and
the SQL behind the transformations.
2. Dependencies and change: source documentation and the effects of source or
schema changes.
3. Testing and contracts: schema and value checks, quality rules, contracts, and
catalogue information.

Day 4 Bitemporal loading and Airflow orchestration
Learning objectives
By the end of the day, learners should be able to:

• Distinguish business-time and system-time history and compare bitemporal loading
strategies.
• Read an Airflow DAG and explain operators, dependencies, parallel tasks, and
callbacks.
• Run and inspect Airflow workflows and explain how Astro blueprints are configured.
• Explain how deployment and monitoring support the product and prepare for the
capstone.

Summary
Open with bitemporal loading and a worked history example, then introduce Airflow and
practise orchestration through the supplied challenges. Follow with deployment and
monitoring demonstrations, and finish by preparing learners for Friday’s capstone.

Bitemporal loading
Work through the presentation in this order:

1. Review truncate-and-reload, insert overwrite, and slowly changing dimension type 2
(SCD2). Introduce business-time and system-time history.
2. Show the organisation’s bitemporal macro and full, incremental, and balance
approaches.
3. Query an associate-history example and trace changes over time. Check the joins
used in the example.
4. Distinguish system load time from business-effective dates, including delivery timing
and time zones.
5. Demonstrate “as of” and “as was” questions, current-record indicators, source
corrections, and audit requirements.
6. Trace the Jinja macro calls and change-detection hashes. Explain which behaviour
comes from the organisational extension.
7. Compare full snapshots and incremental transactions, business keys, ending prior
versions, and inserting new ones.
8. Discuss trading-identity history, balance use cases, and cases requiring custom
strategies.

Check understanding by asking learners which time dimension answers a particular
business or audit question.
Ask: “The source corrects Monday’s value on Wednesday. What was true on Monday, and
what did we believe on Tuesday?” Keep business-effective time separate from when the
correction became known. Use one concrete timeline before opening the macro code. The
temporal reference supplies further examples; the full clinic is separate practice.

Airflow demonstration and practical
Teach the orchestration concepts in this order:

1. Orchestration: coordinate when pipeline components run.
2. Python and operators: define work, then dependencies. Show the Cosmos dbt task
group, other service operators, and Databricks notebook operators.
3. Dependencies and parallelism: explain a directed acyclic graph (DAG). Read task1 >>
task2 >> [task3, task4] as task 1, then task 2, then tasks 3 and 4 in parallel.
4. Configuration and callbacks: explain success, failure, and SLA handling. Show how
logging and failure handling support diagnosis when the pipeline fails.
5. Airflow UI: inspect task states, graph, code, configuration, and logs.
6. Astro blueprints: show reusable Python workflows configured through YAML and
explain when a custom blueprint may be useful.

For Challenge 10, use the Airflow project and its completion criteria. Prepare authentication,
worker paths and the supporting dbt baseline before the timed work. Select the foundation
route; leave advanced recovery work for a separate session. Establish the Python
foundations before moving to blueprint configuration. Encourage learners to inspect task
states and logs as they work, and use the Datadog examples where provided. Collect
unresolved deployment and monitoring questions for the afternoon contributor. Learners
who finish the practical first should explain what would happen if the input were late, the
quality check failed, or the run were repeated. Ask for a log or output check supporting
their answer; use a prepared example if their environment is blocked.
For the supplied example, follow source_ready → dbt_build → quality →
publication_evidence. Ask learners to find the current run, inspect a useful log, and
check the warehouse output. In the controlled failure, quality must block the final evidence
task; removing the cause should allow a checked repaired run.
Ask: “Which run produced this result, and what proves the data is right?” Keep the
scheduled interval separate from the fixture’s business date. A parsed DAG or green graph
alone does not prove the warehouse result. Keep credentials and path errors separate from
the intended data-quality failure.

Deployment and capstone preparation
Use 13:30–14:00 for the deployment contribution. Agree the agenda in advance: where code
goes, how deployment is initiated, where execution is visible, and how failures are
investigated. Keep screenshots ready so the same discussion can run without live access.
After the 14:00–14:15 break, run a release decision exercise until 14:35. Use the platform
review example: a candidate has 500 rows but only 497 distinct IDs, and yesterday’s
accepted output is still available. Give pairs 10 minutes to decide what should happen next
and draft a two-sentence update to the consumer; discuss their answers for 10 minutes.
The failed candidate should remain blocked. Whether yesterday’s output is acceptable
depends on the consumer’s freshness requirement. Ask what information is missing and
who can supply it.
From 14:35–14:50, ask each pair to write a short note about its Airflow failure and repair:
the affected run, useful error, cause, action, output check, and anything still unresolved.
Swap notes for 5 minutes of that time: could another creator locate the run and explain
